A short history of the Imazighen

Imazighen (singular: Amazigh) means “free people”. Tap each period to open it.

Ancient times · Indigenous to North Africa

The Imazighen have lived across North Africa for thousands of years. Rock art in the Tassili n’Ajjer in southern Algeria shows ancient life in the Sahara, and the old Libyco-Berber script is the ancestor of today’s Tifinagh.

c. 945 BCE · Sheshonq I

Sheshonq I, of Libyan (Amazigh) origin, became pharaoh of Egypt and founded the 22nd Dynasty. The Amazigh calendar counts its years from around this date, which is why Yennayer in 2026 marks the year 2976.

c. 202 BCE · Numidia and Massinissa

Massinissa united Amazigh peoples into the kingdom of Numidia, in what is now Algeria and Tunisia. Later Amazigh leaders like Jugurtha resisted Rome, and Juba II ruled Mauretania as a scholar-king.

Roman era · Writers and thinkers

North Africa produced great figures like Apuleius, from Madauros, and Augustine of Hippo, born in Thagaste (today’s Souk Ahras in Algeria). Augustine is widely believed to have had Amazigh roots.

c. 814 to 146 BCE · Carthage and Hannibal

Phoenicians founded Carthage on the coast of today’s Tunisia, and Amazigh kingdoms and tribes around it were sometimes allies and sometimes rivals. Numidian cavalry, famed riders from Amazigh lands, fought for Hannibal and later for Rome, and Massinissa’s support for Rome helped end Carthage in 146 BCE.

5th to 6th century · Vandals and Byzantines

After Rome weakened, the Vandals took Carthage in 439, and later the Byzantines reconquered parts of North Africa. In the mountains and the interior, Amazigh kingdoms and chiefs often ruled themselves.

7th century · Resistance and Islam

As Arab armies arrived, Amazigh leaders such as Kusaila and the queen Dihya (known as Kahina) resisted. Over time most Imazighen embraced Islam, and Amazigh commanders like Tariq ibn Ziyad took part in the move into Iberia in 711.

8th to 12th centuries · Amazigh dynasties

Amazigh-led states shaped the region: the Rustamids of Tahert in Algeria, the Zirids, the Almoravids and the Almohads, who ruled across the Maghreb and al-Andalus. The Kutama of Kabylie also helped bring the Fatimids to power.

1830 to 1962 · Colonial era

France invaded Algeria in 1830. Amazigh figures like Lalla Fatma n’Soumer and the leaders of the 1871 Mokrani revolt in Kabylie resisted. In Morocco’s Rif mountains, Abd el-Krim led a republic in the 1920s. Many Kabyle leaders and fighters, such as Hocine Aït Ahmed and Krim Belkacem, helped win Algeria’s independence in 1962.

After independence · Arabization and identity

After independence, Arabic became Algeria’s sole official language, and Amazigh identity and language were often sidelined. This left many Amazigh people feeling their culture was not recognized.

April 1980 · The Berber Spring

A ban on writer Mouloud Mammeri’s lecture on ancient Kabyle poetry in Tizi Ouzou set off protests across Kabylie. The “Berber Spring” became a turning point for the modern Amazigh cultural movement.

1998 to 2001 · Flag and Black Spring

The Amazigh flag, with blue, green and yellow stripes and the red letter ⵣ (yaz), was adopted by the World Amazigh Congress in 1998. In 2001, the “Black Spring” protests in Kabylie ended with more than 120 people killed and led to major changes.

2002 to 2023 · Recognition

Algeria recognized Tamazight as a national language in 2002 and an official language in 2016, and made Yennayer an official holiday in 2018. Morocco made Amazigh official in 2011, passed an implementing law in 2019, and made Yennayer a national holiday in 2023.

2001 to 2003 · Morocco’s IRCAM

Morocco created the Royal Institute of Amazigh Culture (IRCAM) in 2001, and in 2003 Tifinagh was adopted as the script for teaching Amazigh in schools there.

Today · Still growing

Tamazight is taught in some schools and used in media, but activists say implementation is still uneven. Learning, writing and sharing the language, like in this dictionary, helps keep it alive.

Famous Imazighen

Kings, rebels, writers and artists. Tap a name to read more.

Massinissa · King of Numidia

Amazigh king who united much of Numidia around 202 BCE and built a strong, organized kingdom.

Jugurtha · Numidian king

Grandson of Massinissa who fought a long war against Rome in the late 2nd century BCE and became a symbol of resistance.

Juba II · Scholar-king of Mauretania

Raised in Rome, he ruled Mauretania and wrote books on geography and history. He married Cleopatra Selene, the daughter of Cleopatra and Mark Antony.

Apuleius · Writer

Born in Madauros in today’s Algeria in the 2nd century CE, he wrote the Latin novel known as The Golden Ass, one of the oldest surviving novels.

Augustine of Hippo · Philosopher

Born in Thagaste (now Souk Ahras, Algeria) in 354 CE, he became one of the most influential thinkers in Christian history. Many historians believe he had Amazigh roots.

Dihya (Kahina) · Warrior queen

An Amazigh queen who led resistance in the Aurès mountains in the late 7th century and is remembered as a symbol of courage.

Tariq ibn Ziyad · Commander

Amazigh military leader who led the crossing into Iberia in 711. Gibraltar (Jabal Tariq) is named after him.

Ibn Tumart and Yusuf ibn Tashfin · Dynasty builders

Yusuf ibn Tashfin helped build the Almoravid empire, and Ibn Tumart founded the Almohad movement. Both dynasties were Amazigh-led and ruled Morocco and parts of Spain.

Ibn Battuta · Traveler

Born in Tangier in 1304, he traveled across much of the known world for about 30 years. He is often described as being of Amazigh descent.

Lalla Fatma n’Soumer · Kabyle resistance leader

A woman from Kabylie who led resistance against the French army in the 1850s, often called the Joan of Arc of Djurdjura.

Abd el-Krim · Rif leader

He led the Rif War in northern Morocco against Spain and France in the 1920s and founded the Republic of the Rif.

Hocine Aït Ahmed, Krim Belkacem, Abane Ramdane · Independence leaders

Kabyle figures who played major roles in Algeria’s war of independence from France between 1954 and 1962.

Mouloud Mammeri · Writer and linguist

Kabyle novelist and scholar (1917 to 1989) who collected and studied Amazigh poetry and wrote a Kabyle grammar. His banned lecture in 1980 helped spark the Berber Spring.

Mouloud Feraoun · Writer

Kabyle author of Le Fils du pauvre (The Poor Man’s Son), a famous novel about village life in Kabylie.

Taos Amrouche · Singer and writer

Kabyle singer and author (1913 to 1976) who worked to preserve and perform traditional Kabyle songs.

Idir · Singer

Kabyle singer (1949 to 2020) whose song A Vava Inouva made Kabyle music famous around the world.

Lounes Matoub · Singer and poet

Kabyle singer-songwriter whose songs about identity and freedom made him a major voice of his generation. He was killed in 1998.

Tinariwen · Tuareg band

A band from northern Mali that helped bring Tuareg desert blues to the world and won a Grammy for Best World Music Album in 2012.

Zinedine Zidane · Footballer

French football legend whose parents come from Kabylie in Algeria.
